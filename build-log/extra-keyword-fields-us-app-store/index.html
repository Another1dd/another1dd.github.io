Five Extra Keyword Fields in the US App Store

Every App Store listing gets one 100-character keyword field per language. The US storefront also reads the keyword fields of five other localizations. I didn't trust that until a made-up word put Flare at #1 in the US. Flare 1.5.0 now uses all five fields, which adds up to 500 more characters of English search terms.

The 100-character problem

On the App Store, search matches the app name, the subtitle and a hidden keyword field of 100 characters per localization. For a niche app that is very little room. Flare's en-US field is already full:

perimenopause,night,sweats,hormone,vasomotor,trigger,hrt,estrogen,flush,relief,symptoms,diary,stage

Words in the name and subtitle are already indexed, so repeating them in the field wastes characters. After that, every word that goes in pushes another one out.

The claim, and why I tested it first

Each storefront indexes more than one localization. For the US, the extra ones are Arabic (ar-SA), Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese. A couple of ASO tool vendors reported in 2022 that the keyword fields of those localizations are searchable in the US. That comes from third parties, not from Apple's documentation, and keyword changes only go live with a new version that passes App Review. If the claim turned out to be wrong, I would lose a whole release cycle to find out.

So the first step was a probe, not a rollout.

The probe: one word nobody searches for

Flare 1.3.0 added a Korean listing that copies the en-US name, subtitle, description and screenshots. Its keyword field held a few real English words and one made-up token, qelvarith, which appears nowhere else on the App Store. The rank tracker started watching that token in the US storefront on October 12, and it wasn't ranked.

The day 1.3.0 went live, a US search for the token returned Flare as the only result. It stayed at #1 in every snapshot after that. Since the token existed only in the Korean field, that field is indexed for US search.

Swapping the probe for real words

A made-up word is fine for one test and wrong to keep: App Review Guideline 2.3.7 says keywords must describe the app. So Flare 1.5.0 removes the token and gives each of the five fields one topic, all in English:

Korean: the existing words plus the plurals flashes and flushes. Vietnamese: triggers (caffeine, alcohol, spicy, sugar, smoking). Simplified Chinese: features (intensity, duration, export, weekly digest, correlation). Traditional Chinese: the condition and who has it (menopausal, perimenopausal, midlife, sweating, flushing). Arabic: doctor visits and analysis (appointment, summary, analysis, episode).

The plurals were a separate small experiment. Apple is said to match singular and plural forms on its own. But with no metadata change since 1.2.2, "hot flashes" moved between #4 and #51 in the US while "hot flash" stayed at #1. Adding the plurals explicitly is cheap, and the tracker will show whether they help.

Two rules kept the extra room from being wasted. No word appears in more than one field (en-US included), and every field stays under 100 bytes. One cost: people in Korea, Vietnam, China and Saudi Arabia now see an English listing. For Flare that changes nothing, since the app isn't translated into those languages anyway.

Measuring it properly

A rank change means nothing without a baseline. Before 1.5.0 shipped, US tracking for Flare went from 21 phrases to 48, with every new single word from the extra fields and phrases built from them. The baseline already held a surprise: phrases like "hot flash triggers" (#1), "hot flash diary" (#2) and "hot flash log" (#3) ranked from the existing en-US words alone. The words that live only in the new fields (caffeine, alcohol, journal) didn't rank yet, as expected before the release.

The same five localizations now exist for Kin Compass, Bloomelle, Naveli and Torquelle, each with its own English words.

Where it stands

Flare 1.5.0 went live on November 14. Ranks take three or four days to settle after a release, so the first fair read is around November 18. The question is simple: do the words that exist only in the Vietnamese, Chinese and Arabic fields start to rank in the US? The test token says they should.

The takeaway

When a trick costs a release cycle to try, test the mechanism with something you can measure without doubt before you spend real keywords on it. A token with zero competition answers yes or no in one day. A real keyword moving from #40 to #30 answers nothing.
