What to say to your child after a tantrum

Once the tantrum is over, the temptation is to explain, lecture or ask "why did you do that?". A sequence that tends to work better: wait until your child is genuinely calm, reconnect first, name what happened in a sentence, and ask one simple question about next time. Below is the exact wording, why it tends to help, and what to do when siblings watched.

The script

1Wait for real calm

“Come sit with me. Here's some water.”

Quiet isn't always ready. Wait until your child can make eye contact and take in what you say.

2Restore closeness

“That was hard. I love you. We're totally okay.”

Reconnect before any lesson, so your child doesn't only hear blame.

3Name what happened

“You were very angry when… That makes sense.”

A simple, accepting description of what happened often helps children make sense of a big feeling.

4One simple question

“What do you think would help next time?”

A solution that comes from your child tends to stick better than one handed down, and it gives them some say.

Why it tends to work

CDC's emotion-coaching guidance puts the same order in writing. It says to wait until you and your child are both calm before emotion coaching, and its steps run: notice, connect with eye contact or a gentle touch, listen with empathy and without judgment, name the emotion in simple words, and find good solutions. The script above is those steps in short sentences.

Naming the emotion and then asking for a solution also follows CDC's description of the last step, which is setting limits for behavior while validating feelings: "that makes sense" is the validation, and "what would help next time?" starts the problem-solving.

If your child did something unsafe, the conversation can include a clear, brief limit ("we don't hit"). Keep it to one sentence and keep it warm, so the connection you just rebuilt isn't lost.

When siblings watched

A child who melted down in front of brothers or sisters can feel embarrassed on top of the original upset. Reconnect with them first, then give the room a short, calm frame, then move on:

1Reconnect with the child first

“That was hard. I love you. We're okay.”

Repair the relationship before explaining anything to anyone else.

2Give the room a calm frame

“That was a big feeling. Everyone's okay now.”

A short, neutral statement to everyone present helps stop siblings from turning it into a story.

3Return to normal quickly

“Who's ready for the next thing on our list?”

A fast return to routine tells everyone the moment has passed and isn't the whole day.

What it can sound like

You (once the crying has stopped)Come sit with me. Here's some water.

You (after a few minutes)That was hard. I love you. We're totally okay.

YouYou were very angry when the tablet went off. That makes sense.

YouWhat do you think would help next time?

ChildFive more minutes.

YouLet's try a five-minute warning next time.

Notice that you didn't ask why they did it. "Why" questions can be hard for preschoolers to answer and can sound like blame.

When your child won't talk

Plenty of preschoolers can't or won't answer the question, and that's fine. Skip it, offer a hug or a quiet activity together, and try the "what would help" question another day. Praise the moments they recover well: CDC's guidance on praise says specific praise right after a behavior works best, so "I saw you take deep breaths" tells your child exactly what you liked.

If your child can't settle, hits or hurts themselves, or the meltdowns are frequent and long, bring it up with your child's pediatrician.

What to avoid saying

  • Starting the conversation while your child is still crying or tense. Wait until they are calm.
  • Asking "why did you do that?". It can be a hard question for a preschooler to answer, and it can sound like blame.
  • Turning the talk into a lecture. One sentence about what happened and one question is enough.

Questions

How long should I wait before talking?

Until your child is calm and can look at you, not just quiet. For some children that's a few minutes, for others closer to an hour. CDC's emotion-coaching guidance says to wait until you are both calm.

Should I make them apologize?

A brief repair that fits the situation, such as helping to tidy up or drawing a picture for the person they upset, is often more useful than insisting on a scripted "sorry".

My child has ADHD. Is this different?

These scripts rest on similar principles to the ones CDC says parent training teaches: structure, positive communication and consistency. CDC recommends parent training in behavior management as the first treatment for ADHD in children under 6, and suggests talking to your child's doctor about it. Whether a child has ADHD is a question for a clinician; these scripts are practical communication techniques, not a treatment.
